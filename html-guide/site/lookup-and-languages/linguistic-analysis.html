Linguistic analysis

A dictionary knows what a word can be. An analyzer reads the sentence and says what the word probably is here: its lemma, its part of speech, its grammar, what it belongs to. It can be wrong, so Parseh treats what it says as evidence, and only where you ask.

Everything is on Settings → Linguistic analysis. Getting an analyzer changes no result. You choose what it is used for, one thing at a time.

1. What there is to get#

LanguageAnalyzerWhat it takes
English, German, Spanish, French, Italian, Hindi, PersianUDPipe, one model per languagethe program, a few MB, and a model of 11 to 26 MB
ArabicFarahidi: every reading of a word, and the lemma its sentence choosesabout 12 MB, and half a gigabyte of memory while it works
TurkishStarlang: every reading of a word, without choosingabout 75 MB with what compiling it takes; compiled when installed, so it needs a C compiler
Arabic, TurkishUDPipe as well, for the structure of the sentencea model of 20 or 8 MB

Japanese and Chinese keep the analyzers they have: nothing here changes them. A language you added yourself has none of this, and goes on as before.

Press Get on a row. It fetches only that, from the addresses Parseh knows, and checks each file against its hash. Remove takes it away again, and Remove all of it takes the whole folder. Your books, videos and dictionaries are never touched.

The UDPipe models have a licence of their own. They are CC BY-NC-SA 4.0: free for non-commercial use and shared on the same licence. That is why they are not part of Parseh and are fetched only when you ask. The page and the licences page say whose work they are.

2. What they are used for#

Each is off until you change it, and each can be set by itself.

The dictionary panel.
Show adds a line under each word saying what the analyzer read it as, and whether its sentence chose that. Show and find does that too, and a word the dictionary did not find is looked for under the analyzer’s lemma. A lemma counts only if it lands on a real entry, and the row says that is how it was found. A word the dictionary did find keeps everything it found. If the sentence chose one of its entries, that one comes first.
Cutting new text into phrases.
When a new book or video is cut into chunks, the analyzer’s reading of the sentence is listened to. Your punctuation still comes first, and the text is never changed. Chunks you have edited are never cut again, by this or anything else.
The gloss prompts.
Copy prompt and the skill’s request, for a stretch of a book or a video: each chunk to do carries what the analyzer read in it, with a rule telling the model it is fallible, that the sentence wins, and that it is not part of the answer. Nothing of it is ever stored with a gloss. Your own prompts keep the rule too. After turning this on, download the parseh-gloss skill again, as Settings → Skills for your chatbot reminds you.

A book being made in place by an agent can ask too: while the gloss prompts’ choice is on, the book’s instructions tell the agent about a helper that reads a sentence and writes nothing.

3. Try it#

Try it, at the foot of the page, reads a sentence of yours: every word with what the analyzer made of it, the phrases with and without it, and the dictionary with and without it where the language has one. Nothing is kept. It is the way to see whether an analyzer helps your language before you turn it on.

4. When it cannot help#

If an analyzer is missing, fails, or cannot line its answer up exactly with your text, Parseh does what it did before and says nothing in the page you are reading. Try it says why.

It works on this computer, for whichever device is asking. Nothing is ever sent elsewhere, and a page you are reading offline never tries to run it.

The first request of a language loads its model, about a second; after that a sentence takes a few thousandths of a second. The program goes away after ten idle minutes, with its memory.

5. Is it any good?#

It depends on the language and on the sentence, which is why it stays optional. The models are small and make mistakes a reader would not: UDPipe read wir in a German sentence as a form of werden. Starlang never chooses among the readings of a Turkish word, and Farahidi’s choice of lemma for an unvowelled Arabic word can be wrong while still being one of the word’s real readings. On Linux it has been installed and run. On Windows and macOS it has not been tried yet, Starlang’s compile above all.
