Linguistic analysis

A dictionary knows what a word can be. An analyzer reads the sentence and says what the word probably is here: above all its part of speech. So ran in They ran home is a verb, and the dictionary shows you run and not the noun. It can be wrong, so Parseh shows what it read as a suggestion, and only where you ask.

Everything is on Settings → Linguistic analysis. Getting an analyzer changes no result. You choose what it is used for, one thing at a time.

1. What there is to get#

LanguageAnalyzerWhat it takes
English, German, Spanish, French, Italian, Hindi, PersianUDPipe, one model per languagethe program, a few MB, and a model of 11 to 26 MB
ArabicFarahidi: every reading of a word, and the lemma its sentence choosesabout 12 MB, and half a gigabyte of memory while it works
TurkishStarlang: every reading of a word, without choosingabout 75 MB with what compiling it takes; compiled when installed, so it needs a C compiler
Arabic, TurkishUDPipe as well, for the structure of the sentencea model of 20 or 8 MB

Japanese and Chinese keep the analyzers they have: nothing here changes them. A language you added yourself has none of this, and goes on as before.

Tick what you want, and press Get it: the button says how much it is. It fetches only that, from the addresses Parseh knows, and checks each file against its hash. Remove… on a row takes it away again, after asking, and Remove all of it, under Where it is on this computer, takes the whole folder. Your books, videos and dictionaries are never touched.

The UDPipe models have a licence of their own. They are CC BY-NC-SA 4.0: free for non-commercial use and shared on the same licence. They are not part of Parseh and are fetched only when you ask, so you can use Parseh without them, with nothing of that licence on your computer. The page and the licences page say whose work they are.

2. What they are used for#

Each is off until you change it, and each can be set by itself. The page says in which languages each can work.

The dictionary panel: part of speech.
Show the part of speech puts a chip beside each word saying what it is in this sentence, a line under it with its lemma and grammar, and fits this sentence on the entry of that part of speech. A word’s entries are usually one to a part of speech, so this is the entry that means what the sentence means. Where all a word’s entries are the same part of speech, nothing is marked. Also put what fits first moves that entry to the top, adds it if only other parts of speech had been found, and looks up a word the dictionary missed under its lemma, saying that is how it was found. No entry is ever dropped. The panel needs the language’s own dictionary, from Reading help.
Cutting new text into phrases.
When a new book or video is cut into phrases, the analyzer’s reading of the sentence is listened to. Your punctuation still comes first, and the text is never changed. Chunks you have edited are never cut again, by this or anything else.
The gloss prompts.
Copy prompt and the skill’s request, for a stretch of a book or a video: each chunk to do carries what the analyzer read in it, with a rule telling the model it is fallible, that the sentence wins, and that it is not part of the answer. The sheet says how many chunks carry it, or why none does. Nothing of it is ever stored with a gloss. Your own prompts keep the rule too. After turning this on, download the parseh-gloss skill again, as Settings → Skills for your chatbot reminds you.

A book being made in place by an agent can ask too: while the gloss prompts’ choice is on, the book’s instructions tell the agent about a helper that reads a sentence and writes nothing.

Spoken Persian#

The analyzers learned Persian from written text, and the dictionary’s own reading of spoken Persian knows what a spoken word stands for, so each helps the other. With the part of speech on, every spoken word goes to the analyzer in its formal spelling, so خونشون reaches it as خانه‌شان, a noun, and the sentence around it can be read. Its part of speech then chooses among the spoken readings: بده is “let him give” in پولو بده and may be “it is bad” elsewhere. Which spoken reading a word has is the dictionary’s business, not the analyzer’s: what the analyzer guesses as a lemma for a word it has never seen chooses nothing. Your text is not touched: this formal version is only what the analyzer reads.

What they add, measured#

Fresh sentences written for the purpose, judged by hand and looked up on a full dictionary, with nothing tuned to them: one person’s judgement of a small sample, not a promise about your text.

3. Try it#

Try it reads a sentence of yours: the phrases with and without the analyzer, what the dictionary finds only with it where the language has a dictionary, every word with what the analyzer made of it, and what a prompt would carry. Nothing is kept. It is the way to see whether an analyzer helps your language before you turn it on.

4. When it cannot help#

If an analyzer is missing, fails, or cannot line its answer up exactly with your text, Parseh does what it did before and says nothing in the page you are reading. Try it says why.

It works on this computer, for whichever device is asking. Nothing is ever sent elsewhere, and a page you are reading offline never tries to run it.

The first request of a language loads its model, about a second; after that a sentence takes a few thousandths of a second. The program goes away after ten idle minutes, with its memory.

5. Is it any good?#

It depends on the language and on the sentence, which is why it stays optional. On a set of test sentences in ten languages the marked entry was nearly always the right one, and clearly best on verbs, adjectives and function words. Arabic was the weakest: an unvowelled ضرب was read as a noun in the boy hit the ball, and the noun then comes first. The models are small and make mistakes a reader would not, and Starlang never chooses among the readings of a Turkish word: there it is UDPipe that chooses, among Starlang’s readings. On Linux all of this has been installed and run. On Windows and macOS it has not been tried yet, Starlang’s compile above all.
