The Markdown dialect

A document in Parseh’s studio is a plain text file written in a small Markdown of its own. Most of it is the Markdown you may know already — ## headings, **bold**, lists, tables — and the rest is what a language learner’s page needs and ordinary Markdown has no word for: text in the language you are learning, set in its own face and direction; its pronunciation, riding along unseen until you point at a word; a vocabulary entry with its headword set large; a box, a tint, a colour; a recording cut to one sentence; a formula.

This section describes every one of those constructs, as the studio reads and draws it. It is the reference: what the studio’s parser accepts, what it makes of it on the screen and on paper, and what it quietly leaves out.

1. How to read these pages#

Nearly every construct is shown twice: the Markdown as you would type it, and under it, marked Result, what the studio draws from it. The result is drawn by the studio’s own code — the same parser and the same renderer the reading view uses — so what you see is what your document will look like.

Parseh Markdown
The word کتاب = *book* needs no mark: its script is enough.[این یک PDF است]{tl} is a Persian sentence with a Latin word in it.
Result

The word کتاب = book needs no mark: its script is enough. این یک PDF است is a Persian sentence with a Latin word in it.

Every example is Persian unless it says otherwise, because a document that names no language is a Persian one. An example in another language starts with a short front matter naming it — target: ja, target: it — exactly as a document of that language does.

Where the studio does something this guide’s own pages do differently (the guide also reads Hugo’s Markdown, and some text means one thing to Hugo and another to the studio), the example is shown as source only, and the page says in words what the studio makes of it.

2. Where to start#

How the studio reads a document is the page to read first: it says in what order the studio tries each line, and which parts of ordinary Markdown it has and has not. The cheat sheet then puts every construct on one page, each with a link to the page that explains it. The pages in between take one family of constructs each, and Documents in each language says what changes from one target language to the next.

The exercises — :::exercise blocks, thirteen kinds of them — have a section of their own in this guide, Writing exercises.

  • How a document is readThe order in which the studio tries each line, what of ordinary Markdown it has and has not, and how this guide differs.
  • Cheat sheetEvery construct of the dialect on one page, with a link to where it is explained.
  • Front matterThe five lines at the top of a document — title, subtitle, note, lang and target — and what each one decides.
  • Paragraphs and emphasisLines and blank lines, bold, italic and code — and why emphasis stops at the target language.
  • Headings and vocabularyNumbered sections, subsections, and the lemma heading that sets a word large with its transliteration, origin and meaning.
  • Target-language textHow words of the language you learn are found, set in their face and direction; whole paragraphs, display lines, [...]{tl} and its tints, faces and vertical columns.
  • Colours and pronunciationThe five named colours and any other, a word's transliteration and its kana riding along unseen, and the gloss that feeds the glossary.
  • Boxes and Latin blocksThe > box that holds anything, and the [...]{la} block that sets prose apart — aligned, tinted, narrowed and shifted like a picture.
  • Lists and tablesBullet and numbered lists, one level of nesting, labelled lists, and tables with their alignment, empty cells and sizes.
  • Footnotes and linksNotes cited by name or written in place; links to the web; links to another document by its name, which follow it when it is renamed and wait for it when it is gone.
  • Pictures, recordings, videosThe lines that put a picture, a recording or a YouTube video on the page, their layout in braces, the stretch a recording or a video plays, and what paper makes of them.
  • MathematicsFormulas in LaTeX notation, in the line with [...]{math} or on their own between :::math and :::, drawn on the screen and set by TeX on paper.
  • LaTeX drawingsChemistry, drawings, plots and units drawn by LaTeX itself, in a ::::latex block or inline, [...]{latex}, beside the formulas MathJax draws — with named themes, made in Settings, and a drawing that travels to paper, to a web page and onto a phone.
  • Special charactersThe wrong-form cross, the tick, the arrow, the line break, the zero-width non-joiner and the guillemets — and every button of the editor that types a piece of the dialect for you.
  • What is left outThe Markdown the studio does not read, what it drops without a word, what swallows the rest of a document, and a few rough edges to step round.
  • Documents in each languageWhat changes from one target language to the next — how its text is found, its direction and faces, its options, its vocabulary headings and its transliteration.
