> **This page is a demo.** It shows everything a note can contain, each feature used once or
> twice on real English, with the source open in the editor beside it. Keep what you need and
> delete the rest.

English is both the language this note is written in and the language it teaches, so the studio
cannot tell a word being taught from a word doing the explaining. **Every run of the English
being studied is therefore marked by hand, and nothing is detected automatically**: a word in
square brackets followed by `{tl}` (for *target language*) is English as the subject of the
lesson, set in the note's English face, as [knight]{tl} is here. The **tl** button in the
editor's toolbar wraps the selection this way. An unmarked word is simply prose.

## Plain text

A paragraph is a run of lines: they are joined into one, and a blank line starts the next.
**Two asterisks** make bold, *one asterisk* makes italic, and backticks keep `a piece of code`
exactly as typed. Three asterisks at once do not make bold italic, but italic can sit inside
bold: **a bold phrase with *one word* in italic**.⏎
The editor's line-break button ends a line without ending the paragraph, as it just did.

An arrow is a character of its own, and the editor has a button for it, as it has for the cross
and the tick: [go]{tl} → [went]{tl} → [gone]{tl}. Typed as a hyphen and a greater-than sign, it
comes out the same: [take]{tl} -> [took]{tl} -> [taken]{tl}. A cross marks a wrong form and a tick
a right one: children learning English often say ✗[goed]{tl} for a while before they settle on
✅ [went]{tl}.

### Lists

A hyphen at the start of a line makes a bullet list, and two spaces before the hyphen put an
item one level down:

- English has about forty sounds and only twenty-six letters to write them with.
- A sound can be spelled many ways, and a spelling can stand for many sounds.
  - The vowel of [me]{tl} is also spelled as in [see]{tl}, [sea]{tl}, [field]{tl} and [key]{tl}.
- So the pronunciation is worth writing down for every new word.

A number and a full stop make a numbered list; the numbers are counted for you:

1. the plain form, [write]{tl}
2. the past, [wrote]{tl}
3. the past participle, [written]{tl}

When every item starts with a bold label, the list is laid out as label and explanation:

- **Silent k** [knee]{tl}, [knife]{tl}, [know]{tl}
- **Silent w** [write]{tl}, [wrong]{tl}, [answer]{tl}
- **Silent s** [island]{tl}, [aisle]{tl}
- **Silent t** [listen]{tl}, [castle]{tl}

## English as the subject

### Words and glosses

A marked word followed by an equals sign and a meaning in italics is a gloss: [give up]{tl} =
*stop trying*, [look after]{tl} = *take care of*. The sign is greyed, and the pair goes into the
note's word list (the **Glosses** button of the reading view). The italics say where the meaning
ends. Bold works on marked English too: **[always]{tl}**, **[usually]{tl}** and **[never]{tl}**
stand before the main verb, as in [She always walks to work.]{tl}

In the reading view, hovering a marked word offers to colour it or to give it a pronunciation,
and writes the mark into the source for you.

### Whole sentences

A paragraph that is nothing but one mark is a block of English, set apart in the English face:

[I read the paper every morning, and I read it yesterday too.]{tl}

The same spelling [read]{tl} is said /rid/ in the first half, the present, and /rɛd/ in the
second, the past. (Between slashes, as here, a word is written the way it sounds, in the symbols
of the International Phonetic Alphabet.) A block may take a tint, one of *quote*, *sand*,
*rose*, *sage* and *lilac*; here a tongue twister is tinted *rose*:

[She sells seashells by the seashore.]{tl bg=rose}

A longer block, such as a poem or a dialogue, opens with a square bracket alone on a line and
closes with the mark alone on the last one; the line-break sign keeps each line of it:

[
Tyger Tyger, burning bright,⏎
In the forests of the night;⏎
What immortal hand or eye,⏎
Could frame thy fearful symmetry?
]{tl bg=quote}

William Blake published these lines in 1794, spelling [tiger]{tl} in a way that was already
old-fashioned. The *gh* of [bright]{tl} and [night]{tl} had been sounded a few centuries earlier;
by his day it was silent, as it is now.

[
“Where did you put my keys?”⏎
“I put them on the kitchen table.”⏎
“They aren’t there.”⏎
“Then look in your coat pocket.”
]{tl bg=sand}

Some things other notes do are not needed here. English is written in the same letters as the
prose, so nothing is picked out automatically, and there is no display line, the stand-alone line
a paragraph in another script becomes all by itself. There is no second typeface to switch to,
and no vertical setting: only Japanese and Chinese notes are set in columns.

### Pronunciation

A pronunciation rides on the word itself, after `translit:` inside the braces:
[knight]{translit:naɪt}. It is not printed; hover the word in the reading view to see it. A
colour can share the braces with it: [island]{teal translit:ˈaɪlənd}. English has no separate
reading field; that one belongs to Japanese.

### Colours

Five colours have names^[This page spells the word the British way; in the United States it is
[color]{tl}. A note keeps whichever spelling its source uses.], and each can carry a
pronunciation too. Here the five colours mark five words that share the spelling *-ough* and not
a single vowel sound:
[though]{crimson translit:ðoʊ}, [through]{indigo translit:θru}, [tough]{teal translit:tʌf},
[cough]{violet translit:kɔf} and [bough]{amber translit:baʊ}. Any other colour is written as a
hash sign and six hexadecimal digits: [dough]{#2F6B8F translit:doʊ} rhymes with [though]{tl}.

## Words with entries

A heading whose parts are divided by vertical bars is a word's entry: the word, its
pronunciation, its history and, after an equals sign, a short meaning. The meaning is not
printed; it puts the word into the note's word list. In an English note any heading with a bar
is an entry, so an ordinary heading never contains one.

## thought | θɔt | Old English *þōht*, from *þencan* “to think” | = *an idea that comes into the mind*

- **Noun** an idea or an opinion: [a sudden thought]{tl}, [What are your thoughts?]{tl}
- **Past of think** the past and the past participle are the same word:
  [think]{tl} → [thought]{tl} → [thought]{tl}
- **Sound** the *gh* is silent, and *ou* has the vowel of [caught]{tl}

An entry may leave out its history, keeping just the word and its pronunciation:

## record | ˈrɛkərd | = *a written account kept for later*

- **Noun** the stress falls on the first syllable, /ˈrɛkərd/: [a record of the meeting]{tl},
  [a world record]{tl}
- **Verb** it moves to the second, /rəˈkɔrd/: [to record a song]{tl}
- **Pattern** many pairs work the same way, such as [a present]{tl} and [to present]{tl}

The word itself may carry a colour and its pronunciation in its own mark; the pronunciation
field between the bars is then left empty:

## [light]{amber translit:laɪt} | | Old English *lēoht* | = *what makes it possible to see*

- **Noun** [the light of the moon]{tl}, [Turn on the light.]{tl}
- **Adjective** not heavy, [a light bag]{tl}, or not dark, [a light room]{tl}
- **Verb** [to light a candle]{tl}, past [lit]{tl}

> **Three spellings, one sound**, /ðɛr/:
>
> - [there]{tl} = *in that place*
> - [their]{tl} = *belonging to them*
> - [they're]{tl} = *they are*

## Tables

Columns are divided by vertical bars, and the line of hyphens under the header row says how each
column is aligned: a colon on the left, on both sides or on the right. Two hyphens alone leave a
cell empty.

## Notes at the foot

English spelling settled down in the age of the first printed books, while the sounds went on
changing[^1]. A note's mark is a caret and a name inside square brackets, and its text goes on
a line of its own, anywhere, that starts with the same mark and a colon. A short note can also be
written in place, as a caret followed by its text in square brackets^[Like this one, which adds
that the *i* of [bite]{tl} is said /aɪ/.]. On paper, notes go to the foot of the page; on
screen, they open when hovered.

[^1]: Between about 1400 and 1700 the long vowels of English moved, in what is called the Great
  Vowel Shift. The spelling stayed where it was, which is why [bite]{tl} and [meet]{tl} are not
  said as their letters would be in most European languages.

## Links

A web link is its text in square brackets followed by the address in round ones:
[a short animated film](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aqz-KE-bpKQ), with no dialogue at all.

A link to another note gives that note's name, its title, after `doc:` in the round brackets:
[my next note](doc:My second English note). With nothing between the square brackets it shows
the note's title instead: [](doc:My second English note). No note has that title yet, so both
links are shown as dangling; create a note called *My second English note* and they start to
work.

## Blocks of prose

A paragraph of the prose language can be set apart as a block (the **block** button), with its
own alignment, tint, width and sideways shift. In a note about a right-to-left language, or one
set in vertical columns, this is what keeps a paragraph of prose in its own direction; in an
English note it frames a remark.

[A proverb to take away: [practice makes perfect]{tl}. Here [practice]{tl} takes no article,
because it means practice in general.]{la align=center bg=sand width=80}

[Say a new word aloud three times before you write it down: once the sound is in your ear, the
spelling misleads you less.]{la bg=quote width=80 offset=10}

## Mathematics

A formula is written in LaTeX between square brackets followed by `{math}`. In any long English
text, the word of rank [r]{math} turns up roughly [\tfrac{1}{r}]{math} times as often as the most
frequent one. On a block of its own:

:::math
f(r) \approx \frac{f(1)}{r}
:::

So [of]{tl}, usually the second word in the ranking, appears about half as often as [the]{tl}.

## Exercises

Every exercise sits between a line `:::exercise` with its type and a line of three colons. The
**Exercises** menu of the editor writes any of them from a form, and a **Check exercises** button
at the end of the reading view scores them all.

### Blanks and order

:::exercise fill-blanks
prompt: Complete the sentence with the past of [go]{tl} and of [buy]{tl}. [Both verbs are irregular.]{no-bold}
content-direction: target
text: [Yesterday I [[go]] to the market and [[buy]] two apples.]{tl}
- [go] [went]{tl}
- [buy] [bought]{tl}
- [ ] [goed]{tl}
- [ ] [buyed]{tl}
explanation-correct: Right: [go]{tl} → [went]{tl}, [buy]{tl} → [bought]{tl}.
explanation-incorrect: Neither verb adds *-ed*: the past of [go]{tl} is [went]{tl}, and the past of [buy]{tl} is [bought]{tl}.
:::

:::exercise order-sentences
prompt: Put the steps in order. The first word of each one is the clue.
- [1] [First, wash the apple.]{tl}
- [2] [Next, cut it into four pieces.]{tl}
- [3] [Then take out the core and the seeds.]{tl}
- [4] [Finally, eat the pieces one by one.]{tl}
explanation-correct: [First]{tl}, [next]{tl}, [then]{tl} and [finally]{tl} put steps in order.
:::

:::exercise construct-sentence
prompt: Build the question. A question about the past takes [did]{tl}, and the verb goes back to its plain form.
- [1] [Where]{tl}
- [2] [did]{tl}
- [3] [you]{tl}
- [4] [find]{tl}
- [5] [my keys]{tl}
- [6] [this morning?]{tl}
explanation-correct: [did]{tl} carries the past, so the verb is [find]{tl}, not [found]{tl}.
:::

### Yes or no, true or false

:::exercise yes-no
prompt: Play the recording, then answer each question.
audio: audio/starter-chime.mp3 {width=60 align=center}
- Is the sound in the recording a [chime]{tl}? => yes
- Does [chime]{tl} rhyme with [time]{tl}? => yes
- Is the *ch* of [chime]{tl} said like the *ch* of [school]{tl}? => no
- Is the *ch* of [chime]{tl} said like the *ch* of [kitchen]{tl}? => yes
explanation-correct: [chime]{tl} is said /tʃaɪm/. In many words that came from Greek, such as [school]{tl} and [character]{tl}, *ch* is said /k/.
:::

:::exercise true-false
prompt: Decide whether each statement is true or false.
- [Island]{tl} has a silent letter. => true
- The past of [bring]{tl} is [brang]{tl}. => false
- [Whether]{tl} and [weather]{tl} are said the same way by most speakers. => true
- Apart from modals such as [can]{tl} and [must]{tl}, an English verb has three principal parts, the plain form, the past and the past participle, as in [see]{tl} \=> [saw]{tl} \=> [seen]{tl}; for an irregular verb nothing reliably predicts the last two, so they are learned together with the verb. => true
explanation-incorrect: The past of [bring]{tl} is [brought]{tl}, like [think]{tl} → [thought]{tl}.
:::

### Choosing

:::exercise single-choice
prompt: What is in the picture?
image: images/starter-apple.svg {width=30 align=center}
- [ ] [a apple]{tl}
- [x] [an apple]{tl}
- [ ] [the apples]{tl}
explanation-correct: [an]{tl} comes before a vowel sound: [an apple]{tl}, [an hour]{tl}.
explanation-incorrect: Before a vowel sound the article is [an]{tl}, as in [an apple]{tl} and [an hour]{tl}; before a consonant sound it is [a]{tl}, as in [a pear]{tl} and [a university]{tl}.
:::

An exercise can also keep a picture and a recording back until the answers are checked, right
or wrong: `image-answer:` and `audio-answer:`, as in the next one. The editor's preview, which
shows every exercise solved, shows them at once, and the PDF leaves them out.

:::exercise single-choice
prompt: Which of these words rhymes with [mouse]{tl}?
image-answer: images/starter-house.svg {width=30 align=center}
audio-answer: audio/starter-chime.mp3 {width=50 align=center}
- [x] [house]{tl}
- [ ] [moose]{tl}
- [ ] [mouth]{tl}
explanation-correct: [house]{tl}, /haʊs/, rhymes with [mouse]{tl}, /maʊs/; [moose]{tl} is /mus/, and [mouth]{tl}, /maʊθ/, ends in another sound. The chime stands in for the word said aloud.
:::

:::exercise incorrect-part
prompt: One part of this sentence is wrong. Which one?
- [ ] [My sister]{tl}
- [x] [don't like]{tl}
- [ ] [coffee]{tl}
- [ ] [in the morning.]{tl}
explanation-correct: With a subject that [she]{tl}, [he]{tl} or [it]{tl} could replace, such as [my sister]{tl}, [don't]{tl} becomes [doesn't]{tl}: ✅ [My sister doesn't like coffee in the morning.]{tl}
:::

:::exercise choose-all
prompt: Choose every verb whose past adds a whole syllable, as [want]{tl} → [wanted]{tl} does.
- [x] [need]{tl}
- [ ] [help]{tl}
- [x] [start]{tl}
- [ ] [play]{tl}
- [x] [decide]{tl}
explanation-correct: After a /t/ or a /d/ sound the ending is said /ɪd/: [needed]{tl} /ˈnidɪd/, [started]{tl} /ˈstɑrtɪd/, [decided]{tl} /dɪˈsaɪdɪd/. Anywhere else it adds only a sound: [helped]{tl} /hɛlpt/, [played]{tl} /pleɪd/.
:::

:::exercise odd-one-out
prompt: Which is the odd one out? [Say each word aloud.]{no-bold}
- [ ] [knot]{tl}
- [ ] [wrong]{tl}
- [ ] [lamb]{tl}
- [x] [lamp]{tl}
explanation-correct: [lamp]{tl} is said just as it is spelled, /læmp/. The others hide a letter: the *k* of [knot]{tl}, the *w* of [wrong]{tl} and the *b* of [lamb]{tl}, said /læm/.
:::

### Follow-up questions

A follow-up is a chain of exercises answered one after the other: check a step, read what it says, and the next one comes under it. A step is any exercise, written inside the chain with one colon more on its fences (`::::exercise …` and `::::`). The PDF prints every step at once, numbered, so write each one so that it does not give away the one before.

:::exercise follow-up
prompt: At a café a customer says: [Good morning! I'd like a coffee and a sandwich, please.]{tl} Answer about it, one question at a time.
explanation-correct: You followed the whole order.
explanation-incorrect: Read the sentence once more, slowly: a drink first, then something to eat.
::::exercise single-choice
prompt: What does the customer want to drink?
- [x] [a coffee]{tl}
- [ ] [a sandwich]{tl}
- [ ] [the bill]{tl}
explanation-correct: [A coffee]{tl}; the sandwich is something to eat.
::::
::::exercise true-false
prompt: Now decide.
- [a]{tl} stands before [sandwich]{tl}, because the word begins with a consonant sound. => true
- [Please]{tl} means *thank you*. => false
explanation-correct: [Please]{tl} asks politely; *thank you* is [thanks]{tl}.
::::
::::exercise fill-blanks
prompt: Order for yourself, with the words you have just read.
content-direction: target
text: [Good morning! I'd like [[drink]] and [[food]], please.]{tl}
- [drink] [a coffee]{tl}
- [food] [a sandwich]{tl}
- [ ] [an sandwich]{tl}
explanation-correct: [a]{tl} before a consonant sound: [a coffee]{tl}, [a sandwich]{tl}.
::::
:::

### Flashcards

Flashcards turn over when clicked and are never scored. A word card shows the word and its
pronunciation, then its meaning, with a speaker button for the recording its `back-audio:` names
(the chime, where the word said aloud would go), which plays without turning the card; a card of
opposites shows one word, then the other; the last card is free-form, and each of its four fields
can hold pictures, recordings, lists and almost anything else a note can, exercises apart.

:::exercise flashcard
card-type: vocab
target: [knight]{tl}
transliteration: naɪt
meaning: a soldier of high rank in the Middle Ages, who fought on horseback
context: [The knight rode home through the night.]{tl}
notes: The *k* and the *gh* are both silent, so [knight]{tl} and [night]{tl} sound the same.
back-audio: audio/starter-chime.mp3
:::

transliteration: ˈbɑroʊ

notes: [You borrow from someone, and you lend to someone.]{tl}
:::
